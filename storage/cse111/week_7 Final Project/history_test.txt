XD had always known that his name made people either furrow their brows or smile without quite knowing why. It wasn’t a joke, nor initials chosen by his parents: on his birth certificate, written in blue ink, it simply said XD. To some it was an emoticon; to others, an urban mystery. To him, it was home.

He lived in a room painted a gray that wanted to be blue and with a window that looked out onto a plaza where children flew paper kites. XD worked in Mr. Oliva’s repair shop, a man with blacksmith hands and the patience of a clockmaker. XD fixed radios, bicycles, and, once in a while, broken hearts (he didn’t charge for that). But what he liked most was opening things: old boxes, pendulum clocks, and above all, books locked with tiny padlocks.

One afternoon, while sorting items found on the church roof, XD found a book without a title wrapped in cloth. The lock had no key, only a small inscription: “Laugh and it will open.” XD smiled. It was ironic: someone whose name was the graphic form of laughter now held a book that asked for the same.

He tried everything. He whispered silly jokes, told ridiculous stories about a cat who protested being called “Mr. Whiskers,” until a memory came: as a child, his grandmother tickled him until the air hurt from so much laughing. Remembering that pure laughter, XD began to laugh—not because he expected anything, but because of the memory itself. The book, as if alive, clicked and opened.

Inside were pages full of tiny stories—microtales—that acted like seeds. Each microtale, when read aloud, caused a different bloom: one produced laughter that filled the room with color; another awakened memories of faraway places; another, a sweet nostalgia. XD spent whole nights reading aloud, letting the plaza flood with laughter and healing silences.

People began to notice a change. Customers at the shop returned with brighter eyes, neighbors started sharing soups and secrets, and Mr. Oliva—who always had one brow raised—laughed so hard the neighbor’s cat got startled and then became friends with the corner dog. Laughter was no longer just a symbol in messages; it became a force.

One day a little girl asked XD, “Why is your name like that?” He looked at the book and then at the plaza, where laughter mixed with the wind. “Because being easy to read didn’t interest me,” he said. “I prefer that whoever calls me does it with intention.” The girl nodded and, without hesitation, called him XD aloud, as if saying a promise.

Years later, when someone new arrived in town, everyone told the story of the young man who made books open with a laugh. And although some insisted XD was only an emoticon, those who had met him knew the truth: XD was both a name and a verb; a spark that taught the plaza to laugh at small things. When someone asked about his initials, the old-timers would smile and say, “XD means exactly what you need right now.”

And in his room, the book—closed again—waited for the next laugh that would wake it.
